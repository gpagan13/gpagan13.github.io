Job Market Paper

Bend and SNAP? The Effect of Food Availability on Workplace Injuries
Presentations: WEAI Annual Conference 2026
[PDF]
Abstract

Poverty is closely linked to poor health, but causal evidence on how financial hardship affects working-age adults is limited. I study whether running short of resources raises the risk of workplace injury among low-wage workers, who are concentrated in dangerous jobs and among whom hardship and reliance on the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) are common. The SNAP cycle provides a way to isolate scarcity from other differences between poor and non-poor workers: recipients spend their benefits quickly, and food spending and calorie intake fall until the next disbursal. I study this question in Oregon, where the last digit of the case head's Social Security number determines the day on which the household receives SNAP benefits, allowing me to compare similar workers at different points in the cycle on any given day. Using administrative workers' compensation claims over 2005–2018 and a predicted measure of SNAP eligibility, I find no detectable cycle when occupations are weighted equally, but injury rates are about 5.7 percent higher in the last ten days of the benefit month than in the first six when occupations are weighted by injury risk. This increase is concentrated among men aged 18 to 24, whose injury rates rise by 28.5 percent in the second half of the benefit month, and, less precisely, among women aged 45 to 54. The composition of injuries also changes: on the day benefits are received, the rate of the most severe injuries rises by about 28.1 percent while less severe injuries are unchanged, a shift that changes in hours worked would not produce. These intent-to-treat estimates likely understate effects for SNAP participants by a factor of 2.1 to 3.2.

Working Papers

Ethnicity and Agricultural Attachment in the Post-WWII Southwest
Presentations: AAEA Annual Meeting 2026, EHA Annual Meeting 2026
[PDF]
Abstract

World War II (WWII) was a large and multifaceted labor market shock that catalyzed improvements in the economic outcomes of Black men in the United States. Manufacturing booms, labor shortages, and federal efforts to lower employment barriers for underrepresented groups pulled men off the farm and into factory jobs, which led to persistent economic gains. The same can not necessarily be said for Hispanic men in the Southwest, of whom over 20 percent were employed in agriculture still in 1950. This paper investigates the drivers of Hispanic men's relative attachment to the farm sector in the Southwest during this pivotal period in history, with a particular focus on the role of introducing the Bracero program. Using linked census data from 1940 and 1950, I find that Hispanic men were more likely to enter the agricultural sector than Black men by 1–2 percentage points. I also show evidence consistent with two potential mechanisms: language entry barriers in the non-farm sector and increasing demand for Spanish-speaking farm workers. In ongoing work, I explicitly consider the role of the foreign workers in shaping the occupational choice of natives, and I explore whether this attachment to the farm sector played a role in the economic trajectory for Hispanics in the Southwest.
