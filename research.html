Bend and SNAP? The Effect of Food Availability on Workplace Injuries
Presentations: WEAI Annual Conference 2026
[PDF]
Abstract

I leverage exogenous variation in the timing of SNAP benefit disbursal to estimate how relative food availability affects workplace injuries. SNAP recipients spend most of their benefits in the first two weeks after disbursal, and prior research shows that this pattern, known as the SNAP cycle, affects cognitive performance and health, but its effect on worker safety has not been established. Using administrative workers' compensation claims from Oregon over 2005–2018 and a predicted measure of SNAP eligibility, I find no effect when workers are pooled, but large and offsetting effects by gender among workers aged 18 to 24. Injuries for young women are highest in the first days after benefits arrive, while injury rates for young men rise in the second half of the benefit month by 17.6 percent for non-severe and 25.4 percent for severe injuries. Because severe and non-severe injuries move proportionally, these patterns are most consistent with changes in hours worked. Among men aged 45 to 54, by contrast, the rate of severe injury rises by 47 percent on the day benefits are received while non-severe injuries do not, a shift that labor supply alone would not produce. These intent-to-treat estimates likely understate effects for SNAP participants.

Working Papers

Ethnicity and Agricultural Attachment in the Post-WWII Southwest
Presentations: AAEA Annual Meeting 2026, EHA Annual Meeting 2026
[PDF]
Abstract

World War II (WWII) was a large and multifaceted labor market shock that catalyzed improvements in the economic outcomes of Black men in the United States. Manufacturing booms, labor shortages, and federal efforts to lower employment barriers for underrepresented groups pulled men off the farm and into factory jobs, which led to persistent economic gains. The same can not necessarily be said for Hispanic men in the Southwest, of whom over 20 percent were employed in agriculture still in 1950. This paper investigates the drivers of Hispanic men's relative attachment to the farm sector in the Southwest during this pivotal period in history, with a particular focus on the role of introducing the Bracero program. Using linked census data from 1940 and 1950, I find that Hispanic men were more likely to enter the agricultural sector than Black men by 1–2 percentage points. I also show evidence consistent with two potential mechanisms: language entry barriers in the non-farm sector and increasing demand for Spanish-speaking farm workers. In ongoing work, I explicitly consider the role of the foreign workers in shaping the occupational choice of natives, and I explore whether this attachment to the farm sector played a role in the economic trajectory for Hispanics in the Southwest.
